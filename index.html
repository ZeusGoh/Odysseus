      Signal history

      Every cross in the loaded history, scored by what price did next.

      How to read this

      What it is. Every stochastic cross sitting in the loaded price history is replayed as if you had taken it, then scored by what price actually did over the next —. Nothing here is a prediction — it is a record of what already happened.

      Positive always means the signal was right. Returns are direction-adjusted: for a bear cross, price falling counts as a positive number. So you never have to work out which way to read a minus sign.

      Hit is the share of those signals where price went the right way. Move is the median result — the middle one, with half better and half worse. Median rather than average, because one violent move would otherwise make a mediocre group look excellent.

      Why these distances. A (5,3,3) stochastic travels from oversold to overbought in roughly four bars, so a cross has finished playing out within a handful of bars. Scoring it further out than that stops measuring the signal and starts measuring whatever happened afterwards — which is why longer horizons flatten every row toward a coin flip.

      A bar is not the same length on every row. The columns are counted in bars, but a bar is an hour on 1H and a month on 1M — so "7 bars" means seven hours on one row and seven months on another. Each row prints its own real span next to the frame, and hovering a row spells out every column in full. The badge under a live cross uses the real span directly, so on a daily cross it reads 1d, 3d, 5d, 7d rather than bars.

      What a good row looks like. A hit rate above about 55 with a positive median is a signal worth respecting. A hit rate near 50 with a clearly positive median means a handful of large winners are carrying it — playable, but only if you let winners run. A positive hit rate with a negative median is the opposite trap: often right, badly wrong when it fails.

      Sample size decides everything. Under about 30 signals, treat a row as a rumour — those rows are dimmed. The last — bars of every chart are unscored, because a cross needs a future before it can be judged, and the still-forming bar is never scored at all.

      Rows are ranked frame-relative. A percentage on the monthly and a percentage on the hourly are not the same kind of number, so sorting the setup table on raw % would just sort by bar length. Each setup is ranked instead by how far it beats an ordinary cross on its own frame; hover a row to see that multiple.

      What this does not model. Fees, funding and slippage are all ignored, so thin edges are flattered. Signals also overlap — crosses can fire a few bars apart, so two rows may be scoring much of the same price move and the sample is not as independent as the count suggests. And it is one exchange, one coin, and only the history currently loaded.

        How these signals are read

        Confirmed versus potential. Confirmed means the cross printed on a bar that has already closed, so it cannot be taken back. Potential means it sits on the bar still forming: %K is through %D right now, but if it slips back before that bar closes the signal disappears. Divergence splits the same way, since a run whose latest leg rests on an unclosed cross can also unwind.

        Divergence. Read on geometry rather than on where it sits on the scale. Bullish joins price lows while the stochastic makes higher lows, bearish joins price highs while the stochastic makes lower highs, at 50 just as readily as at 10 or 90. Heights come off %K alone. A line is void if %K cuts through it between the two ends, and where several swings in a row keep diverging they join into one run.
